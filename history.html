History of the Mountain

From an Iroquois hunting trail to a strategic thoroughfare in the Civil War to the site of an aviation disaster, our area is rich in history. BRMCA is working to share information about historic events, people, and places.

Bear’s Den Rural Historic District

Bear’s Den Rural Historic District (BDRHD) is a national historic district in Bluemont, across Clarke County and Loudoun County. It encompasses 152 contributing buildings, 12 contributing sites, eight contributing structures, and one contributing object.

The district includes a collection of late 19th and early 20th century dwellings constructed as summer homes by wealthy Washingtonians attracted to the mountain’s cooler climate. The architecture reflects a number of popular styles, primarily American Craftsman or Bungalow, Colonial Revival, and Queen Anne.

Other contributing buildings include farm outbuildings, barns, and stables; domestic outbuildings such as spring houses, meat houses, guest cottages, and root cellars; a former school, and a former church.

The contributing sites include the ruins of buildings, including picnic shelters, above-ground cisterns, and an old road bed. The contributing object is a county boundary marker.

BDRHD is on the National Park Service’s National Register of Historic Places (2008) and the Virginia Landmarks Register (2009).

BRMCA is working with Clarke County to establish a Historical Highway Marker for the district.

TWA Crash on the Mountain

On Dec. 1, 1974, a Trans World Airlines Boeing 727 on approach to Washington, D.C., crashed into the side of Mount Weather, killing all 92 people on board.

TWA flight 514 was scheduled to travel from Indianapolis to Washington National Airport, with a stop in Columbus, Ohio. Bad weather in the nation’s capital forced the crew to reroute to Dulles. But tragedy struck when, according to the National Transportation Safety Board review, the plane descended too low too soon.

A series of catastrophic human errors, including a miscommunication between pilots and air traffic controllers, combined with heavy fog and snow to bring about the disaster.

In the aftermath, the NTSB recommendations accepted by the industry changed confusing airport approach charts, cleared up contradictory terminology that pilots and air traffic controllers used to communicate, and insisted that ground proximity warning systems go on all commercial aircraft within the year.

All told, TWA flight 514 was one of the most influential accidents in aviation history.
